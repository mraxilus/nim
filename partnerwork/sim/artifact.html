The body sim

Two bodies of the average adult's measurements — a torso of its tape round, a neck and a head stacked on it — and arms of three links on a shoulder that swings and twists, an elbow that bends and a wrist that bends any way, each joint with the range a dancer tolerates. Pick which hands are held and at what level, and turn either dancer a quarter at a time — on their own axis, or in orbit round their partner — and the arms take the most comfortable pose that holds, where the solver puts them, not where a drawing would like them. Keep turning and a joint runs out, and the page says which. Nothing here stands for anything; it is the model, drawn.

Below the plan is the same pose from the side, looking across the line between the two bodies, which is where an elbow's height and which of two crossing arms is over can be seen. A quarter is walked in small moves, so an arm that has gone round a body stays round it, and where no small move holds the turn stops: the tally shows how far it got and the page says what blocked it. The bodies are the average adult's and do not change. How far apart the couple stand is not a control either: they stand, and go on standing as they turn, wherever the joints are furthest from their limits, stepping in or out a centimetre at a time; the hands prefer to be between the two bodies, on the line between their centrelines, at whatever height within their level leaves the joints freest. An orbit keeps whatever side of the walker faced the centre facing it, so a follow's quarter in orbit lands where the lead's quarter on axis lands. Changing the hands or the level settles the pose afresh, face to face. The sweep button turns the follow from that rest until a joint runs out, at the distance the couple settled to there, and is the authority for where a turn blocks.
