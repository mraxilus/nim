Whole cloth

Everything agreed so far, redrawn from the ground up. Six ideas, in the order each one needs the last: the marks, the heights, the holds, the modifiers each arm can wear, the turning that writes them, and the moves between states. The four conventions you have accepted are kept exactly — the chevron-circle body, the two arm inks with a deep shade for the lead, square against round, and the level fills. Everything else is re-derived from your definitions and marked for what it is: agreed, measured, or proposed.

plate 1 · the marks

Four conventions, kept exactly

These are settled, so everything after this plate is drawn in them and nothing restates them. A dancer is a circle with a chevron for the facing. A hand is its arm's ink — blue for left, orange for right — worn deep by the lead and plain by the follow; the lead's hands are squares, the follow's are rounds, so ink says which arm, shade and shape say whose. The fill on a hand is a height, defined on the next plate.

plate 2 · the heights

A level is a height, and nothing else

Your definitions place the three levels on the body itself: low is below the shoulder, about the torso; high is above it, about the neck; above clears the head. A level says where a connection rides — it says nothing about which arm passes over which; that distinction belongs to the wrap, on plate 4. Because the pages are drawn from overhead, height is carried by the fill.

Above is different in kind, and the drawing says so: a hand above sits over the head — the centre of the circle — on the very axis the couple turns about. That placement is why plate 5 will find it turning without limit, and why it can never wrap or lock.

plate 3 · the holds

Six holds, and the empty one

A hold names which hands are joined — nothing more. Written as lead–follow with case carrying who: L–l is the lead's Left to the follow's left. Facing each other, a same-name hold crosses the midline by nature and a cross-name hold runs parallel; the drawing shows it rather than a word.

The over-variants are folded into one hold, per your lean. Your sheet lists L–l over R–r and R–r over L–l separately and doubts the split; this page treats which arm is over as a fact of the wound state — the same hold half a turn along its own chain — and says it with the crossing break, not with a new name. The break is the only over/under mark on the page, and it always means the same thing: the broken line passes under.

plate 4 · the modifiers

What one arm can do, at its height

A modifier belongs to one arm of one dancer — either dancer. Your definitions give each modifier a different anatomy at each height, so there are four shapes, drawn here on a follow's left arm; capitalise the caption and the same four belong to a lead. A lock goes round the back; a wrap crosses the front — and which of her arms it passes under or over is exactly what tells a low wrap from a high one, so her other arm is in the picture where a wrap needs it.

Notice what carries each fact: where the hand sits says lock or wrap (behind the body, or across its front); the fill says the height; the break says under or over. Nothing is written twice, so nothing can disagree.

plate 5 · turning

Turning is what writes the modifiers

A position is a hold worn at an orientation — the same joined hands, with one dancer turned some number of quarters. And orientation is not decoration: turn the held dancer and the connection winds across her body, which is where wraps and locks come from. Your one worked row says it for L–l held low: half a turn one way is the wrap; a whole turn the other way is the lock.

The dial: one hold, turned, at each height

For L–l, turning the follow. Filled pills are states that hold; the dashed pill is a hand led behind — the shape of the lock without its bind, which arrives at the whole turn. Mirror the whole dial for R–r.

What is whose on this dial. The low row's wrap-at-a-half and lock-at-a-whole are your sheet's, verbatim. The extents — where ✕ begins, the extra room at high, above turning free — are the rope model's measurements, offered for validation, not asserted. And the dial surfaces a vocabulary question your sheet hasn't answered yet: is a led hand already a lock? The shape is there at the half turn; the bind is not until the whole. If the dance treats them as different states, they may deserve different words.

plate 6 · the moves

Two primitives, four named compounds

Everything that changes what is held reduces to two primitives; the four starred helpers are worth their names because a lead thinks of each as one action. The starred ones keep contact through a trace — drawn dashed along the body, since a trace is a hand travelling on the partner, not through the air.

collect primitive

Join a connection: a lead hand takes a follow hand.

drop primitive

Release it: the connection ends, both hands go free.

flick compound *

A drop with momentum: the hand is sent, not just let go.

trace compound *

Slide a held connection along the partner: contact, moving.

place compound *

Collect, then drop somewhere new — traced, with nothing in the way.

cut compound *

Drop, then collect past an obstruction — an arm, torso, or neck in the way; the trace carries it over.

Where the safety edge lives: among these arrows. Any move that would produce lock@low must arrive from a low carriage — collect low and turn, or trace down first. The state itself is fine; the forbidden thing is one particular way in.
