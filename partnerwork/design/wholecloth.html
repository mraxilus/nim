Whole cloth

Everything agreed so far, redrawn from the ground up. Six ideas, in the order each one needs the last: the marks, the heights, the holds, the modifiers each arm can wear, the turning that writes them, and the moves between states. The accepted conventions are kept exactly — the chevron-circle body, the two arm inks with a deep shade for the lead, square against round, and the level fills. Everything else is re-derived from your definitions and marked for what it is: agreed, measured, or proposed.

plate 1 · the marks

The conventions, kept exactly

These are settled, so everything after this plate is drawn in them and nothing restates them. A dancer is a circle with a chevron for the facing. A hand is its arm's ink — blue for left, orange for right — worn deep by the lead and plain by the follow; the lead's hands are squares, the follow's are rounds, so ink says which arm, shade and shape say whose. The fill on a hand is a height, defined on the next plate.

a dancer — the chevron is the facing; the rim is the body the arms go round left arms — blue; the lead's deep and square, the follow's plain and round. A mark is an arm's anchor, fixed at the body's side; it never moves right arms — orange, the same way round a connection — two arms meeting end to end. The rope of a mark's colour is that arm, and its end is the hand; the seam where the two ropes meet is the joined hands, the deep rope always the lead's. Straight wherever it can be; it leaves a mark at its edge so the fill stays readable faded — a free hand: nobody holds it, but it keeps its identity

plate 2 · the heights

A level is a height, and nothing else

Your definitions place the three levels on the body itself: low is below the shoulder, about the torso; high is above it, about the neck; above clears the head. A level says where a connection rides — it says nothing about which arm passes over which; that is said by the drawing itself, on plate 4. Because the pages are drawn from overhead, height is carried by the fill.

Above is different in kind, and the drawing says so: a hand above sits over the head — inside the circle — on the very axis the couple turns about. That placement is why plate 5 will find it turning without limit, and why it can never wrap or lock.

plate 3 · the holds

Six holds, each at its own rest

A hold names which hands are joined — nothing more. It is written lead to follow, case carrying who: Left to left is the lead's Left holding the follow's left. Every hold is drawn at its rest — the orientation where nothing is wound. For the cross-name holds that is face to face; for the same-name holds it is the follow turned away, where the connection runs clean down one side. At rest, every connection is a straight line.

Why the follow-away rest matters. Drawn this way, Left to left at rest is the same shape as Left to right at rest — only her facing, and so which of her hands, differs. The two two-hand holds become one geometry offset by her facing, and their twist chains line up: the X above is either pair wound half a turn, not a hold of its own.

And the over-variants fold away with it, per your lean. Which arm is over in the X is a fact of the wound state, said by the crossing break — the broken line passes under. That break is used between two connections only; two arms of one body say their over/under differently, on the next plate.

plate 4 · the modifiers

What one arm can do, at its height

A modifier belongs to one arm of one dancer — either dancer — and it rides inside that hand's own name: Left-Lock-Low to right is the lead's Left, locked low, holding the follow's right. Two drawing rules carry the anatomy. An arm goes around the outside of the body circle — only an above hand may sit inside it, and never over the chevron. And when two arms of one body overlap, radius decides: the inner arm hugs the body, the outer arm hugs the inner, and the outer one is on top. No breaks needed — you read the track. The mark never moves: what moves is the rope, and the hand is where the rope ends — a low lock ends just behind the opposite shoulder, a high lock behind its own, a wrap at the opposite front. Each figure was asked of the rope model.

What carries each fact: the track says lock or wrap (round the back, or across the front); where the rope ends says which lock (opposite shoulder low, own shoulder high); the radius says under or over her other arm; the fill on the fixed mark says the height. Nothing is written twice, so nothing can disagree.

plate 5 · turning

Turning is what writes the modifiers

A position is a hold turned some number of quarters from its rest — face to face for the cross-name holds, follow away for the same-name ones. And orientation is not decoration: turn the held dancer and the connection winds across her body, which is where wraps and locks come from. Your one worked row says it for Left to left held low: half a turn one way is the wrap; a whole turn the other way is the lock.

The dial: one hold, turned, at each height

For Left to left, turning the follow from her away rest — measured on the rope model from that rest, not from face to face. Each marker names what her arm has become at that turn, in the sheet's words; the legend below says how to read them. Mirror the whole dial for Right to right.

What is whose on this dial, and one thing to reconcile. Your sheet's row says wrap at ½, lock at 1. Counted from face to face the rope agrees exactly: wrap at a half one way, the wound low lock at a whole turn the other. Counted from her away rest — the default you gave the same-name holds — the rope finds the same two states at ½ either way, because the away rest already sits a half turn along that chain. So either the row is counted from face to face, or the lock is a half turn from rest; the drawing above takes the second reading and the choice is yours.

The extents — where ✕ begins, the extra room at the neck, above turning free — are the rope model's measurements, offered for validation, not asserted. The earlier "led" marker is gone: what the rope showed at that half turn is the hand behind her own shoulder, which is your high lock's place, not a state of its own.

plate 6 · the moves

Two primitives, four named compounds

Everything that changes what is held reduces to two primitives; the four starred helpers are worth their names because a lead thinks of each as one action. The starred ones keep contact through a trace — a hand travelling on the partner, not through the air. Definitions only for now: the symbols for these are deliberately unchosen, and the next round will offer candidates.

collect primitive

Join a connection: a lead hand takes a follow hand.

drop primitive

Release it: the connection ends, both hands go free.

flick compound *

A drop with momentum: the hand is sent, not just let go.

trace compound *

Slide a held connection along the partner's body: contact, moving.

place compound *

Collect, then drop somewhere new — traced the whole way, with nothing in the path.

cut compound *

Drop, then collect past an obstruction — an arm, torso, or neck in the way; the trace carries the contact over it.

Where the safety edge lives: among these moves. Any move that would produce a …-Lock-Low hand must arrive from a low carriage — collect low and turn, or trace down first. The state itself is fine; the forbidden thing is one particular way in.
